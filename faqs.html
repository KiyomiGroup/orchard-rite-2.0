Frost protection, answered

Frequently Asked Questions

The questions growers ask most often about wind machines, frost fans, and how to run them well.

You gain an advantage in numbers. Wind machines and frost fans can be deployed in far greater numbers than overhead sprinklers, heaters, or air drains.

They protect crops by raising temperatures. On radiation-cooling nights, they pull down warmer air and ventilate the growing area, preventing cold air from pooling and preventing buds from supercooling — by up to 4°F (2.2°C) on still, clear nights.

They duplicate nature’s own protection: the wind. Crop damage happens when the wind stops. Generating air flow prevents that.

They’re easy to manage — available on demand, programmable to start and stop as conditions change, and movable to new blocks when replanting takes place.

No — the opposite. On cold, calm nights, plant surfaces radiate heat and become colder than the surrounding air. Wind machines warm those surfaces by moving air across them every five minutes.

We recommend starting your frost protection equipment 4°F (2.2°C) above the critical temperature for the crop being protected.

The temperature expected to inflict at least 10% mortality in the buds, fruits, canes, or wood, depending on the crop. For tree fruit like apples, pears, peaches, or apricots, published bud-stage tables identify the expected 10% kill temperature. Where those don’t apply, freezer testing of wood can determine it.

On nights when the forecasted low is more than 4°F (2.2°C) below the critical temperature — especially when skies clear early, wind dies early, and the dew point runs 5°+ (2.75°+ C) below critical. The wind machine circulates that heat through the block and between machines to keep it from dissipating.

On the side of the block where cold air infiltrates the protection zone — behind ditch banks, road beds, and low areas. Without it, temperatures on the drift edge fall as the machine struggles to keep the zone above critical.

  • Under-tree sprinklers — running large blocks (5+ acres) at roughly 40 GPM/acre gives an additional ~2°F (1.1°C) of lift.
  • Heaters — diesel, propane, wood, coal, or hay-fired pots can add another 2–4°F (1.1–2.2°C) of lift, depending on the system.

Yes — if the temperature rises 4°F (2.2°C) above critical (clouds moving in) or wind reaches a steady 10+ MPH (16+ KPH), the wind itself is providing equivalent protection.

If conditions stay clear with variable wind, keep the machines running even at temperatures well below critical — they’re still preventing supercooling.
